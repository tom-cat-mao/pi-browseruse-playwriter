How Tidal Bores Reshape Estuaries

By Dana Whitfield · November 2, 2024 · 9 min read

A tidal bore is the leading edge of an incoming tide travelling up a river against the current. It looks like a single breaking wave, sometimes only a few centimetres high, yet it moves more water than a small river carries in an hour.

Researchers have tracked bores in the Severn, the Qiantang, and the Amazon for decades. The measurements agree on one point: the bore is not just a curiosity of fluid dynamics, it is a sediment pump.

Why sediment matters

Sediment carried by a bore settles in slack water behind the wave front. Over a season the deposits raise the bed of the channel, which in turn changes where the next bore breaks. The feedback loop is slow but persistent.

  • Coarse sand settles first, within hundreds of metres of the front.
  • Fine silt travels further and builds the fluid mud layer upstream.
  • Organic matter binds the deposits once vegetation returns.

Measuring the front

Field teams use pressure transducers and acoustic Doppler profilers to log the passage of a bore. A typical deployment returns a velocity profile such as u(z) = u* / k * ln(z / z0), which is then fitted to a log law.

"The bore does the work of a dredger, and it does it for free. The problem is that we never asked where it puts the material."

What happens next

Estuary managers now model bores explicitly rather than treating them as noise in the tidal signal. The third-generation bore model couples the wave front to bed roughness, and early results suggest the coupling halves the error in long-run deposition forecasts.

The remaining question is biological. If the fluid mud layer thickens, does the benthic community recover between bore seasons, or does it need several quiet years?
